Sharks, at a glance

Sharks is an educational exploration game that invites people to learn facts about sharks through playable experience. Players choose a character, swim through an illustrated ocean and discover 18 short facts inside fluffy white bubbles. Flags preserve those discoveries, while environmental encounters introduce marine habitats and pollution. The intention of the game is to give people a friendly way into a subject they might associate with fear.

01 · Problem and user needs

Visiting shark exhibitions made me reconsider the fear I had associated with sharks. I wanted to create a game that encouraged people to approach them with the same curiosity I felt during those visits.

When revisiting the original game, I noticed that the experience did not always support that intention. The shark could be difficult to locate on a phone, facts were hard to read, and touch gestures sometimes triggered more than one action. I focused the refinement on making exploration easier to follow, so players could spend more attention on what they discovered.

The design question: how could players explore at their own pace while understanding where they were, how to move and what they had found?

I had casual players with little prior shark knowledge in mind. Reviewing the prototype helped me identify three priorities for their experience. These were starting assumptions to guide the design, rather than findings from user interviews:

  • Know where I am. Find my character and follow its movement.
  • Feel in control. Move and stop when I expect to, including after reading a fact.
  • Understand what I found. Read a fact comfortably and know how to find it again.

02 · Process

Research and inspiration

The concept drew on museum visits and educational material from the American Museum of Natural History, New York Aquarium and Smithsonian Ocean. These references introduced me to the diversity of sharks and their role in marine ecosystems, which became the starting point for the game’s content.

I also considered how sharks are represented in games such as Maneater and Hungry Shark World. Their focus on action and feeding helped me clarify a different direction for Sharks: a relaxed experience where learning something new is the main reward. This comparison informed the concept; understanding how players respond to it will require user research.

Building the experience around discovery

I organized the experience around a simple sequence: choose a shark, explore, discover a fact and continue. The three characters share the same controls, allowing players to choose one they like without comparing abilities. A field guide gives them a more direct way to revisit what they have learned.

Wireframes and information hierarchy

Each screen has a different role. Character selection introduces the controls, the ocean view helps players stay oriented, and the fact card gives them space to read. These simplified layouts show how I prioritized information in each part of the journey.

Refining the playable prototype

Working with a playable prototype helped connect the screen designs to movement and feedback. I refined how the shark responds to input, how facts open and close, and how discoveries are remembered. Automated browser checks covered these interactions on desktop and emulated phone layouts.

The checks confirmed that the interactions behaved as expected. The next step is to observe first-time players and see whether the controls and feedback make sense without explanation.

03 · Solution

1. Helping players stay oriented

The problem: on smaller screens, the character could be difficult to find. The change: the camera follows the shark, its size adjusts to the available screen space, and a YOU label distinguishes it from other creatures. The layout also leaves room for the header and controls.

I removed the extra circles around characters and objects to keep the ocean visually uncluttered. Camera tracking and the label provide the guidance instead. Rotating the phone also resets movement input, giving players a stable starting point in the new orientation.

2. Making room for reading

I separated actions that had been competing with one another. Dragging moves the shark, while a dedicated button handles spinning. When a fact opens, the ocean pauses and movement input is cleared. Players can then read without worrying about where their character is going.

Touching a bubble opens its fact immediately, connecting the encounter to a moment of learning. Because this interrupts swimming, I kept the card brief and easy to close. In usability testing, I would explore whether players prefer this immediate response or an option to open the fact themselves.

3. Giving discoveries a lasting place

I wanted players to recognize a discovery after closing its card. A found bubble becomes a flag, and the Found message explains that they can return to read it again. The discovery count and field guide keep track of the same collection, so revisiting a fact does not add to the total.

On small screens, swipe to read all columns. You can also focus the table and use arrow keys.

A consistent visual language

The shark and assets illustrations set a playful tone. I carried that into the interface through an ocean-blue background and warm yellow actions, with pale surfaces and dark text for easy reading.

Content hierarchy: each fact card follows the same order: title, short explanation, discovery status and return action. Reusable components: character cards and buttons use consistent spacing and visual treatment. Interaction states: visible keyboard focus and scrollable cards help the interface work across input methods and screen sizes.

Bringing the interactions into code

Contributing to the code helped me work through details that a static layout could not show: what happens when a finger lifts, the screen rotates or a player returns to a saved game. These details became part of the interaction design.

The ocean is built with p5.js and p5.play, while menus and fact cards use HTML and CSS. Keeping the reading interface separate from the canvas makes text layout and scrolling easier to manage as the camera moves. Discoveries are saved in the browser; cross-device progress is outside the current scope.

04 · Outcomes and next steps

The refined prototype brings character selection, exploration, reading and completion into one continuous experience. Browser checks covered all three characters at four viewport sizes—320 × 568, 390 × 844, 844 × 390 and 1366 × 768—using desktop Edge with phone emulation.

What I learned

This project helped me see how closely visual clarity depends on interaction behavior. A readable fact card still needs a reliable way back to the game; a progress marker needs to make sense after its message disappears. Working on both the design and code helped me follow those decisions through the whole experience.
